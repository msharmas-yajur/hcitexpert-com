> **The Research Radar** · 17 November 2026 · Issue 002
> An alternate-day briefing on the ten highest-ranked recent preprints in digital health and clinical AI, plus a personal health records spotlight and a new section on biomedical research agents, written for India's health-IT community. Preprints are early work. We say what the authors report, and where it stops.

**In this issue**

- **InclusionMed**, the one paper this issue ranked by Hugging Face upvotes, is an open evaluation initiative. Its authors report index scores from 17.5 to 49.1 out of 100 and, per the full text, say the first release evaluates 22 models and has no benchmark for patients and the public.
- **Several of the ten papers are evaluations or audits of models or benchmarks**, for example InclusionMed (a benchmark index), an audit of mental health recommendations from 41 language models, and a leakage audit of a chest X-ray benchmark.
- **A personal health records spotlight**: three preprints, and three live PHR or health-record apps, one each from India (Eka Care), the Middle East (Shifa, Oman) and the US (Apple Health), described from their operators' own pages.
- **New: biomedical research agents.** Three preprints on agents used for biomedical science, a review, an automated chemical synthesis system and a computational benchmark. They are research tools, not clinical care.

**Preprint notice.** These are arXiv preprints. They have not necessarily been peer reviewed, and results may change. We report what the authors state in their abstracts and, where marked, in their full texts. None of this is clinical guidance.

## The ten papers

The papers are numbered by shortlist rank, 1 to 10. No candidate was skipped on reading.

### 1. [InclusionMed: Benchmarking AI for Inclusive Healthcare](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21218)

The InclusionMed Collaboration · arXiv:2611.21218 · 16 November 2026 · **Rank basis:** Hugging Face upvotes (58, as of 17 November 2026, 15:23 UTC)

- **What it does:** an open medical AI evaluation initiative. Its atlas organises benchmarks across three healthcare roles and three AI capabilities. From 388 candidate benchmarks the authors selected four public benchmarks, and added a private diagnostic reasoning benchmark of their own, then aggregated results for frontier language models into an index.
- **What it reports:** overall index scores range from 17.5 to 49.1 out of 100 (full text: across 22 models from 11 families), and rankings differ by role and capability. On one benchmark (HealthBench Professional, full text), all ten top-ranked models scored higher on patient communication than on clinical decision-making.
- **Caveat:** the abstract says the five benchmarks cover three of the atlas's nine cells. Per the full text, there is no benchmark for patients and the public, the private benchmark is graded by a language model, and the authors say suitability for a clinical deployment still needs validation in the intended setting. The PDF header shows it is a technical report, version 0.5.
- **Why it could matter in India (our reading):** an index like this can help a buying team see which role and capability a benchmark actually tests before they rely on a headline score.

### 2. [Evidence-Conditioned Completion and Synthesis of Electronic Health Records](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19546)

Sicheng Shu, Parker Knight, Huitao Li et al. · arXiv:2611.19546 · 14 November 2026 · **Rank basis:** not featured on Hugging Face, ranked by keyword relevance

- **What it does:** EHRDiB, a diffusion-bridge model that either completes a partly observed EHR visit or synthesises a new one, while varying how much of a patient's real information is supplied as input, and measures both downstream usefulness and disclosure risk.
- **What it reports:** on MIMIC-IV and Duke Health data, the authors report that for synthesis the gap in a prediction metric (AUPRC) against real data is 45.2% smaller on ten MIMIC-IV targets and 53.3% smaller on numerical Duke data than the best comparator, and that a membership-inference attack score rose from 0.516 to 0.598 as more patient evidence was supplied.
- **Caveat:** per the full text, the Duke data are not shareable, the authors say the method offers no formal privacy guarantee, results have no confidence intervals, the work covers single visits only, and code is promised on publication. Preprint.
- **Why it could matter in India (our reading):** the privacy result is a reminder to test synthetic records for leakage before anyone treats them as safe to share.

### 3. [CONTEXT-PCa: Evaluating the role of clinical context in MRI-based AI for detecting clinically significant prostate cancer (study protocol)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19082)

Shiqi Huang, Weixi Yi, Shaheer Ullah Saeed et al. · arXiv:2611.19082 · 14 November 2026 · **Rank basis:** not featured on Hugging Face, ranked by keyword relevance

- **What it does:** a study protocol. The authors plan to test whether adding clinical variables to a published image-only AI model, with its weights fixed, improves detection of clinically significant prostate cancer on bi-parametric MRI. The abstract describes a cohort of 6,022 cases from 4,540 patients at University College London Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust, 43 clinical variables, a primary endpoint of the difference in AUROC, and five external cohorts. The full text calls it a retrospective cohort study.
- **What it reports:** no results. It describes the planned design.
- **Caveat:** a protocol, so there are no findings to cite. Per the full text, the authors list limitations such as a single tertiary centre, verification bias, labels that include presumed-benign cases, and that patient overlap with the image model's development cohorts cannot be excluded.
- **Why it could matter in India (our reading):** a published protocol is a template for how a hospital could pre-register an evaluation of a clinical AI tool before running it.

### 4. [Multimodal Fusion: A Review of Breast Cancer Advanced and Traditional Modalities](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18793)

Jawaher Al-Tuweity, Joao Paulo Papa · arXiv:2611.18793 · 13 November 2026 · **Rank basis:** not featured on Hugging Face, ranked by keyword relevance

- **What it does:** a review of imaging modalities used in breast cancer detection and of AI-based multimodal fusion.
- **What it reports:** per the full text, the authors searched a literature database for 2017 to 2025, identified 1,050 papers plus 2 from citation search, and included the 50 their ranking placed highest. They state gaps in the literature: small private or single-centre datasets, limited prospective multicentre evidence and limited interpretability.
- **Caveat:** a review. Its statements describe the literature it reviewed, not new evidence. The arXiv record gives no peer-review status.
- **Why it could matter in India (our reading):** the gaps the authors list are a checklist of questions to ask a vendor of a multimodal breast imaging tool.

### 5. [Understanding Users' Security, Privacy, and Trust Concerns in Healthcare AI Systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20223)

Tushar Nayan, Mohammed Al Duniawi, Song Fang et al. · arXiv:2611.20223 · 15 November 2026 · **Rank basis:** not featured on Hugging Face, ranked by keyword relevance

- **What it does:** an analysis of discussions in 14 medical Reddit communities to see what security, privacy and trust concerns people express about healthcare AI.
- **What it reports:** the abstract cites 6,514 relevant posts, which the full text explains are threads a classifier labelled as security, privacy and trust related. Per the full text, 46.02% of the labelled discussion fell in a "Trust and Responsible AI" theme, and around the events the authors timed, 5 of 12 AI development events, 2 of 5 privacy incidents and 4 of 5 regulatory events showed a statistically significant change in discussion.
- **Caveat:** per the full text, Reddit users are self-selected and pseudonymous with unverifiable roles, the data are English only, much labelling is automated, and the authors describe changes in discussion as coinciding with events. The arXiv record gives no peer-review status.
- **Why it could matter in India (our reading):** it samples English-language online talk, so it is a prompt for a local survey of patient trust, not a substitute for one.

### 6. [From AI-Enabled Remote Patient Monitoring Toward Agentic Robotics: Embodiment, Governance, and Care Integration](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18484)

Ivana Ragusa, Lorenzo Guiducci, Filippo Cavallo · arXiv:2611.18484 · 13 November 2026 · **Rank basis:** not featured on Hugging Face, ranked by keyword relevance

- **What it does:** a scoping review of AI-enabled remote patient monitoring systems, which then proposes "agentic robotics" as a supervised, auditable and interruptible direction.
- **What it reports:** per the full text, the review covers 93 core system papers drawn from 516 records. Of these, 4 (4.3%) reached sustained real-world deployment, 22 (23.7%) reached the two higher levels of decision-action coupling, and 16 (17.2%) were embodied systems.
- **Caveat:** the abstract proposes agentic robotics as a future direction, and the paper is a review that reports no test of it. Per the full text, the authors say the embodiment finding applies only to the monitoring corpus and should not be generalised to healthcare robotics as a whole. The arXiv comment says it is accepted for publication in Annual Review of Control, Robotics, and Autonomous Systems, Volume 10 (2027).
- **Why it could matter in India (our reading):** the deployment count is a useful sanity check on vendor claims about autonomous home monitoring.

### 7. [Curbside Chatbots: Auditing the Mental Health Interventions Large Language Models Recommend to Clinicians, Caregivers, and Patients](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12814)

Matthew Flathers, Jill Noorily, Phuong Anh Nguyen et al. · arXiv:2611.12814 · 9 November 2026 · **Rank basis:** not featured on Hugging Face, ranked by keyword relevance

- **What it does:** an audit of what 41 language models recommend for mental health conditions, using vignettes written in three voices (clinician, caregiver and patient).
- **What it reports:** the abstract gives 19 vignettes and 35,055 responses. Per the full text, the vignettes are 17 conditions and 2 controls, and a language-model judge extracted 494,328 interventions from the responses.
- **Caveat:** per the full text, the vignettes were written by the authors without patient data, there is no answer key, the judge model is itself one of the audited models, queries went through US accounts in American English, and the voice effect is confounded with whether the diagnosis was named. The PDF header says the paper is a preprint not yet peer reviewed, and the dataset is promised on publication.
- **Why it could matter in India (our reading):** it uses English-language prompts sent from US accounts, so a clinician-led check in Indian languages is the open question for any mental health assistant here.

### 8. [Target Recoverability under Selective Labels: A Failure Mode in ECG-Based Acute Coronary Syndrome Prediction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11063)

Alexander Schubert, Anton Hakansson, Ziad Obermeyer · arXiv:2611.11063 · 8 November 2026 · **Rank basis:** not featured on Hugging Face, ranked by keyword relevance

- **What it does:** a study of how training only on tested patients can teach a model associations that fail or reverse in untested patients when a diagnosis target includes cases the input cannot show. The authors illustrate it in simulation and in ECG data.
- **What it reports:** per the abstract, among patients who were not tested, three ECG foundation models fine-tuned on revascularization scored AUROC 0.381 to 0.478 (below 0.5) for 30-day major adverse cardiovascular events, while models trained on ECG-visible subtypes scored 0.680 to 0.709 and a model trained on unstable angina scored 0.310 (below 0.5).
- **Caveat:** per the full text, the authors call 30-day major adverse cardiovascular events an imperfect proxy for potentially missed acute coronary syndrome. The arXiv comment says it is accepted to the Proceedings of Machine Learning for Health (ML4H) 2026.
- **Why it could matter in India (our reading):** any hospital training on "who got tested" labels could ask the same question of its own data.

### 9. [Leakage- and Label-Aware Benchmarking of Class-Imbalance Strategies for Multimodal Chest X-Ray and EHR Classification](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07950)

Dang-Khoa Bui, Quang-Huy Tran, Minh-Khoi Nguyen-Bui et al. · arXiv:2611.07950 · 5 November 2026 · **Rank basis:** not featured on Hugging Face, ranked by keyword relevance

- **What it does:** re-audits an earlier benchmark on MIMIC-CXR and MIMIC-IV, shows that splitting at record level leaked patients across splits, then re-benchmarks with patient-separated splits and refined labels.
- **What it reports:** the abstract says about a third of radiographs carry labels that differ from the codes of their own admission. The abstract reports that, under patient-separated splits and in the evaluated configurations, no class-imbalance strategy raised the observed mean macro F2 over no augmentation by more than 0.01.
- **Caveat:** per the full text, the refined labels are not clinically verified, the study uses one cohort from one hospital system, and the authors say the operating points do not establish screening value. Preprint.
- **Why it could matter in India (our reading):** it is a short checklist for any team about to quote a validation score from a public imaging benchmark.

### 10. [How Deep is the Signal? Time-Context up to 30 Years for Colorectal Cancer Prediction in a Primary-Care EHR Model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07265)

Achille Raviez, Lan Wang, Brendan Delaney · arXiv:2611.07265 · 5 November 2026 · **Rank basis:** not featured on Hugging Face, ranked by keyword relevance

- **What it does:** tests how far back in a patient's history a primary-care EHR foundation model should look when predicting colorectal cancer, from 2 to 30 years, against a gradient-boosting baseline. The abstract names North West London primary care.
- **What it reports:** the abstract gives an AUROC of 0.551 at 2 years, 0.590 at 18 years and a baseline peak of 0.589. Per the full text, the transformer's AUROC was 0.580 at 30 years and the baseline peaked at 14 years.
- **Caveat:** per the full text, cases and controls are matched on age and sex, so the authors say the figures are not comparable with clinical tools such as QCancer, the authors list a single-region study, and the data are not public. The arXiv comment says it is accepted at ML4H 2026, Findings Track.
- **Why it could matter in India (our reading):** this prompts asking how much history a model needs before paying to digitise decades of paper records.

## Patterns across the ten

- Several of the ten are evaluations or audits of models or benchmarks, for example InclusionMed (a benchmark index), Curbside Chatbots (an audit of 41 language models) and the leakage audit of a chest X-ray benchmark.
- Three of the ten are not new experiments: a review (breast cancer fusion), a scoping review (remote patient monitoring) and a study protocol (CONTEXT-PCa).
- InclusionMed's authors report that of the 338 benchmark tasks where they could identify a geographic setting, 318 were associated with the United States, and that 88% of the 388 candidate benchmarks included English (full text).

## Personal health records spotlight

Personal health records (PHRs) and the apps that carry them are where patients meet their own health data. This section pairs three recent preprints with three live PHR or health-record apps, one each from India, the Middle East and the US, the three markets we are tracking. Only one of the three preprints is about a PHR as such. The other two are about patient-facing AI. The apps are described from their operators' own pages, and we have not tested them.

### Three preprints on PHRs and patient-facing health AI

These three came from a separate shortlist (see "How this issue was made"). None was featured on Hugging Face, so all were ranked by keyword relevance.

#### [Medical Fine-Tuning Reshapes, Not Reduces, Patient-Facing AI Risk: A Rubric-Based, LLM-Judge Evaluation Framework](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24807)

Savan Doshi · arXiv:2610.24807 · 17 October 2026 · **Rank basis:** not featured on Hugging Face, ranked by keyword relevance

- **What it does:** proposes a rubric that scores language model answers to patient questions by risk-bearing language, such as treatment directives, dosage expressions and overconfident assertions, rather than by factual correctness, and applies it to six open models, three general and three medically adapted.
- **What it reports:** the abstract describes a rubric of six risk categories applied to six open models (three general, three medically adapted) on 200 real patient questions from the K-QA benchmark, and says two of the six categories had low agreement among the language-model judges. Per the full text, the six models form three medical-general pairs, mean overall risk scores differ negligibly across them (within 1.24 to 1.43 on a 0 to 5 scale, with overlapping 95% confidence intervals), and medical fine-tuning changes the profile of risk by category.
- **Caveat:** per the full text, severity is scored by language-model judges, clinician calibration is described as ongoing, and the factuality comparison uses only 50 of the 200 questions. The arXiv record gives no peer-review status; the PDF header says it was presented at the DAIH Workshop at COLM 2026.
- **Why it could matter in India (our reading):** it is an example of testing the kind of language a patient-facing assistant uses, not only whether its facts are right.

#### [CG-Probes: Recovering Guardrail Directions from Patient Query Embeddings](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31062)

Marko Rehacek, Vitezslav Dusek, Martin Rusinko et al. · arXiv:2609.31062 · 25 September 2026 · **Rank basis:** not featured on Hugging Face, ranked by keyword relevance

- **What it does:** trains small probes on frozen text embeddings of Czech oncology search queries to score risk on three axes in patient queries (medical urgency, psychological urgency and topic sensitivity), and compares them with language model graders.
- **What it reports:** the abstract describes probes competitive with open-weight language models on urgency axes. Per the full text, one axis was not recovered by a single direction, the probes missed none of 28 silver-labelled emergencies but escalated 19.5% of 133 benign queries, and only the frontier model separated clearly, on psychological urgency.
- **Caveat:** per the full text, about 65% of the highest-grade medical urgency evaluation queries and 97% of the highest-grade psychological urgency queries are synthetic, the two annotating oncologists are co-authors, and the probes are not deployed. The arXiv comment says it is accepted as a short paper at CIKM '26.
- **Why it could matter in India (our reading):** guardrails for patient queries will need local languages and local escalation paths, which this Czech study does not test.

#### [Review Before Trust: Source-Grounded Integrity Gates for AI-Assisted Personal Health Records](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29965)

Nora Girda, Adrian Groza · arXiv:2608.29965 · 30 August 2026 · **Rank basis:** not featured on Hugging Face, ranked by keyword relevance

- **What it does:** a gate for a personal health record application in which values extracted from medical documents by a language model stay provisional until a deterministic monitor checks them against the source text. The authors say it evaluates system integrity rather than clinical correctness or clinical safety.
- **What it reports:** the abstract reports a replay on nine laboratory reports with 102 manually labelled rows, in which a schema-only policy admitted 97 numeric candidates, while the stricter quote-and-row gate admitted 72 and sent 25 to review.
- **Caveat:** per the full text, the data are nine reports from one adult member of the research team, labels were not clinician-adjudicated, extraction used a hosted model with no zero-retention claim, and a refusal does not show a value is false. The arXiv record gives no peer-review status; the PDF carries a PSCare 2026 proceedings header.
- **Why it could matter in India (our reading):** any ABDM-linked app that digitises lab reports with AI could ask where a value is held until someone has checked it.

### Three PHR and health-record apps in the wild

## Biomedical research agents

New from this issue. These are preprints on AI agents and foundation models used in biomedical science, such as drug design and chemistry. They are **research tools, not clinical care**. We say what each paper does, what the authors report, and what kind of evaluation it was, from the full text. Three preprints came from a separate shortlist (see "How this issue was made"). None was featured on Hugging Face, so all were ranked by keyword relevance.

### 1. [AI for Biologists: The Knowledge Boundaries of Foundation Models in the Age of Agentic Systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17015)

Tian Cai, Andrew Kleist, Jaimin Patel et al. · arXiv:2611.17015 · 12 November 2026 · **Rank basis:** not featured on Hugging Face, ranked by keyword relevance

- **What it does:** a review. It explains how biological foundation models are built, proposes five design elements that it says define a model's "knowledge boundary", and proposes standardised descriptions of model limits.
- **What it reports:** no results of its own. Per the full text, it quotes other studies, for example one in which, on variant classification from DNA, a language model alone reached 49% and 80% with an added DNA model.
- **Caveat:** evaluation type: review, with no experiments, simulation or wet lab. Per the full text, the paper proposes a "boundary statement" for each model and a community-maintained registry, and the acknowledgments disclose AI assistance in literature collection, fact verification and language editing. The arXiv comment says it is to be published in Annual Review of Biomedical Data Science.
- **Why it could matter in India (our reading):** the idea of a model card for what a biology model cannot do is easy to borrow for any research group choosing tools.

### 2. [Vibe synthesis: hierarchical agentic decision-making for intent-driven multistep molecule synthesis](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18772)

Jie Chen, Guikun Chen, Feng Wan et al. · arXiv:2611.18772 · 12 November 2026 · **Rank basis:** not featured on Hugging Face, ranked by keyword relevance

- **What it does:** VibeSyn, a hierarchical agent system that turns chemist-defined molecular objectives into decisions executed through computational tools and laboratory automation.
- **What it reports:** per the full text, in a campaign reproducing a 10-step synthesis of catharanthine first reported in 1987, human input accounted for 7.3% and human confirmation for 4.2% of 722 recorded actions. For the cyclic peptide campaigns the figures were 7.5% and 5.2%.
- **Caveat:** evaluation type: wet-lab demonstrations of chemical synthesis. Per the full text, there is no baseline comparison, humans submitted tasks, a human chemist ran one step by hand, and nothing was tested biologically or clinically. The arXiv comment says "30 pages, 5 figures".
- **Why it could matter in India (our reading):** it shows what "human in the loop" can mean in numbers for an automated laboratory.

### 3. [Pyrite: Building and Benchmarking Protein-Ligand Pose Ranking Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14511)

Megha Srivastava, Michael H. Lim, Allison M. Keys et al. · arXiv:2611.14511 · 10 November 2026 · **Rank basis:** not featured on Hugging Face, ranked by keyword relevance

- **What it does:** a benchmark of 1,500 pairs of computed ligand poses, and two language-model agents that rank candidate poses pairwise.
- **What it reports:** the abstract reports agent accuracies of up to 67% and 74%, exceeding observed expert performance. Per the full text, on the 265-pair curated split with human annotations, five expert chemists reached 0.45 accuracy counting abstentions as wrong and 0.57 on decisive answers only, and a plain logistic regression scored 0.64.
- **Caveat:** evaluation type: computational (in silico) benchmark against public crystal structures, with no patients and no wet lab. Per the full text, the candidate poses come from the authors' proprietary model, the task is pairwise and the authors list transfer to real ranking pipelines as future work. The arXiv comment says it is a NeurIPS 2026 Agentic AI for Biological Discovery Workshop paper.
- **Why it could matter in India (our reading):** a plain logistic regression scored 0.64 on the same curated split (the agents reach up to 67% and 74%), so asking for simple baselines before paying for an agent is a reasonable habit.
